On the need to be loved

In each of us, beneath our outer appearance, there throbs, almost unmentionable, the desperate need to be recognized, approved, loved. A vital need when we are children, and one that, alas, takes us a very long time to soothe. Who can say the pleasure of being, thanks to age and maturity, somewhat freed from it.

Femina, 1995
✦

The Mask We Wear

To be recognized. To be approved. To be loved

Three desires that silently govern most of our choices. The way we enter a room. What we say and what we swallow back. The people we approach. Those we avoid. The sovereignty of these desires manifests from the very first days of our existence — even before we have words to name them. We need it.

He who pretends not to like praise wants to be praised twice.

As we grow up, we conceal this need beneath a mask designed to silently negotiate our place in the gaze of others.

Not out of calculation. Not through deliberate lying. We simply cannot step naked before judgment and beg for love. The raw, unadorned interior is too fragile to be seen.

We adjust. We smooth things over. We do not present ourselves as we truly are — out of the need to be approved, recognized, loved by others. And this need alone is enough to transform us, even before others start look.

Self-Portrait with Masks — James Ensor, 1899

Menard Art Museum, Komaki, Japan — Public domain

In this painting, James Ensor paints himself alone at the center of a crowd of grimacing masks. He alone appears without a mask — his human face, slightly anxious, contrasts with the grotesque and theatrical faces that surround him on all sides. The work says: we live surrounded by masks, often without knowing which one is our own. Ensor confessed: “I have shut myself up in the land of mockery, where everything is but a brilliant yet violent masquerade.” »

We forget the mask so quickly that we eventually confuse it with our own face. That is where everything becomes complicated. When we no longer quite know what we are hiding… or even whether we are hiding anything at all.

Let us ask ourselves the question. Beyond what others see of us, beyond even what we believe we show and what we keep secret — what remains that we truly know about ourselves? Does the desire to be recognized not arise precisely from that which, within us, still escapes all knowledge?

I do not know what I am ; I am not what I know.

The Childhood We Never Truly Leave

As a child, this need is legitimate. It is even vital..

A child who is not loved does not survive. Not only in the physical sense but in the sense of what makes a human being become a human being — someone who feels entitled to exist, to take up space, to want things.

The child is not ashamed of this need. He does not hide it. He stretches out his arms. He cries. He demands. And it is right — it is his nature. He depends entirely on the other to understand that he has value.

But something is supposed to happen as we grow up. This need is supposed to calm. Not disappear — but no longer rule everything. We are supposed to gradually reach a place where the absence of approval no longer destroys us. Where the gaze of others ceases to be the only mirror available. This passage is called maturity.

It is not a question of age. Nor of intelligence. Nor of success either. Maturity is deeper. Harder to name. A kind of reconciliation with oneself… a way of no longer needing others to confirm who we are.

Fear, Dissatisfaction and Exile

Behind the smooth façade — polished like a pebble that has rolled for a long time in the water — lies a deep dissatisfaction. Never quite fulfilled. Always waiting. Always turned toward something yet to come.

So we go out. We seek company. We hope to meet the other who will fill this void. And sometimes we think we have found it — and then it falls apart. Even more painfully than before.

We live in fear. And that is how we do not live.

This fear is not the fear of great catastrophes. It is the fear that the other’s gaze will be a gaze of disappointment. We carry it everywhere. It is fused with our way of being. We no longer even feel it — just as we no longer feel the weight of our own clothes.

It is perhaps this strange mixture of fear of loss, inability to be fully satisfied with what we have, and lack of self-knowledge that gives rise to the feeling that religious and philosophical traditions call exile.

Not the exile from a country, a house, or a family. But the exile from oneself. A diffuse, permanent feeling, rarely admitted — of being slightly beside who we truly are. As if our most intimate interior were waiting for something it has never been given.

Dependence on Others

According to certain psychological readings, there are two great dependencies that structure our inner life.

The first is the need for approval — to be recognized, valued, admired. This is the figure of the father, in its broadest sense : the authority that validates, that says you have done well, that you are capable, that you deserve your place.

The second is the need for love — to be loved unconditionally, without having to earn it. This is the figure of the mother : love that demands nothing, that does not judge, that is there simply because we are there.

These two needs do not disappear as we grow up. They shift. They disguise themselves. They crystallize onto others — colleagues or superiors whose opinion obsesses us, loves from whom we hope to be the absolute priority, strangers whose words or glances are enough to color — or darken — an entire day.

No love can take the place of true love.

Glory does not replace love. The approval of crowds does not replace inner peace. And the One — that other person we seek all our lives to be loved by in an exclusive and definitive way — cannot bear all that weight either.

Love That We Confuse with Desire

There exists a fundamental asymmetry between what man seeks in love and what woman seeks. Not universally. Not absolutely. But often enough to deserve being named.

The boy plays at love for which he is not ready, because he desires the body. The girl plays at the body for which she is not ready, because she desires love.

This is not an accusation. It is a misunderstanding that perpetuates something eternal between human beings. Two different demands that cross paths, sometimes miss each other, and sometimes, miraculously, meet.

But as long as the demand remains a demand — as long as I love you means love me — there is not yet true love. There is expectation disguised as a gift.

No one is less worthy of love than he who lives only to be loved.

What Maturity Is Not

We believe that maturity means no longer feeling the need. That accomplished adults no longer need to be loved, approved, or recognized.

This is not the case.

Maturity is understanding the need. Recognizing it. No longer being ashamed of it. And no longer letting it entirely govern our choices. It is a difference that changes everything.

It is not about becoming indifferent. Not about hardening ourselves, building walls, or deciding that others no longer matter. It is about no longer depending on their gaze to know who we are. Self-knowledge — even theoretical, even incomplete — is already a beginning.

Knowledge contributes but little to salvation. That little contains much.

The Path of Return

Certain traditions speak of birth. Not the first — the one that does not depend on us — but a second birth. The one that is chosen, or that arrives, or that we allow ourselves to receive.

These are not gradual stages. Not a smooth, linear progression. It is closer to a reversal. To a moment when something turns within.

And the condition for this reversal is perhaps simply having learned to remain alone. With oneself. Without fleeing into noise, activity, or the gaze of others. Not solitude as punishment. Solitude as space. As a place where we eventually find ourselves again.

It is not that others no longer matter. But that the source we seek outside — in gazes, in loves, in successes — is already there, silent and patient, within.

I searched the whole world. And the beloved was waiting for me in my own house.

Note on sources

These reading notes seeks to simply convey reflections and lines of thought attributed to various thinkers and scholars, from different religions, traditions, and cultures, around universal inner experiences. It is above all a meditative reading.
